# GPU work

The rules for GPU kernel and native-runtime work live in this skill. The evidence behind them lives in the research repo, and runbooks live in private-workbench. Two shorthands are used below:

- `R` = `~/code/research/gpu-kernels/`: results per project, and the general lessons file `R/cuda-kernels/cuda-kernel-lessons-general-2026-10-04.md`. "Lessons §N" means its section N.
- `PW` = `~/code/private-workbench/`: the hill-climb playbook, the worker brief template, and one sheet per repo with its build, bench, accuracy gate, helper scripts and rejected ideas.

## How to run the work

1. Read "Be ambitious", "Every project" and "Kernels" below, then the track or tracks that fit in "Pick your track". Open only the notes they link.
2. Start the project's decision log before the first design choice, and keep it up as you go: [references/recording.md](references/recording.md) "Decision log".
3. Speed climb on an existing pipeline, or a port's speed phase once its parity gate passes: open `PW/playbooks/gpu-hill-climb.md` and the repo sheet `PW/ops/<repo>/hill-climb/README.md` before the first speed change, and follow its loop: nsys to rank the gaps, then ncu on each one you act on.
4. Delegating to a worker: fill `PW/playbooks/kernel-worker-brief-template.md`, copying in only the rules that apply and naming the ambitious design to build first.
5. All GPU work, including tests, nsys and ncu, goes through the homebased GPU queue; never run it directly. Before queuing, import every module each benchmark venv loads on the CPU, and after a job read each step's own exit status: a job reports success when its wrapper exits 0, and three LivePortrait jobs (hours of queue each) died on missing Python packages behind a green job.
6. At the end of each phase, after a revert or a parked attempt, and after the user corrects you: log the decision, then record results and lessons as in [references/recording.md](references/recording.md).

If you are a worker with a brief, the brief replaces steps 1, 3 and 4. Put your decisions and rule candidates in your report rather than the research repo or this skill; the root records them. The ambition rule still applies.

## Be ambitious

Agents drift to small, safe tuning (parameter sweeps, launch configs, small fusions) because it's easy to measure. That is the second pass, not the plan.

- **Build the gap-and-floor table on day 0.** For each case, list the hardware floor (bandwidth or FLOPs), our time and the target. Measure the floor rather than quoting it: notes have disagreed on the 5090's tensor rate by 2x. Add the achievable ceiling next to it, read from ncu: what the vendor's best kernel for that shape reaches (its tensor-pipe or DRAM active %). In the sam3rs video tracker, a peak-FLOPs floor put the BF16 GEMMs 8.4 ms away from it; ncu had cuBLASLt at 83-87% tensor-active, so most of that gap wasn't there.
- **Where a component is far from its floor, build the design that can reach it.** If the target is more than ~1.3x away, or ours runs below ~70% of the floor, the default is a rewrite; tune only within ~15% of the floor. Spend about 70% of the budget on one or two such rewrites and 30% on tuning and integration. In OKC the big gains came from rewrites, while a day of tuning landed inside the judge's ±2-3% noise. Tuning still pays as a second pass (scriptrs #14 gained 9.8-14.1% with no rewrite). (`R/cuda-kernels/okc-tuning-vs-rewrites-lessons-2026-10-25.md`, lessons §5a and §7)
- **Judge a rewrite by its ablation ceiling** and drop it only for a proven blocker, never for its first end-to-end number. The same threshold decides go/no-go verdicts on whole native runtimes: a measured ceiling past ~1.3x over the best existing configuration is a go, not "tune the incumbent first". Docling's assessment first called a 3-4x native ceiling (10-20x over deployed defaults) a no-go, then "kernel first", before the user corrected it (`~/code/research/inference/specialized-model-runtimes/docling-baselines-2026-11-01.md`). A climb that only tunes has skipped the big rows.

## Every project

- **First cut the work needed for the same result.** Get the same output, within the accuracy gate, from less computation and data movement: remove, precompute, reuse or combine work. Then speed up what remains. This means less work for the GPU, not less effort from you: an algorithm change that removes work is often the ambitious rewrite. Verify outputs and measure total workload time. ([Evidence](~/code/research/inference/gpu-economics/deepinfer-lessons/gpu-optimization-review-2026-10-31.md))
- **Measure the end-to-end payoff before building anything.** Profile the whole pipeline on the target GPU and do the Amdahl arithmetic: how much of the end-to-end time can this stage move? Then confirm with a rough prototype. A proof harness built before that check cost speakrs five days and shipped one PR; the rough check (same output, measured speedup on a fixed input set) shipped more in the next eight hours. Baseline against the best existing deployment, not defaults: worker processes under CUDA MPS (threads serialize on the GIL), and open upstream patches. In Docling, an open TableFormer KV-cache branch plus 10 processes under MPS took 12.1 to 70.4 pages/s with no new kernels, which ruled out a native runtime (`R/cuda-kernels/speakrs-no-cudnn-lessons-2026-10-07.md`; `~/code/research/inference/specialized-model-runtimes/docling-baselines-2026-11-01.md`)
- **Diagnose before you pick a design.** nsys says where the time goes and the floor says how far it is; neither says why. Before writing the hypothesis for a gap you act on, run ncu (`--set full`, playbook §3) on its kernel and cite the reading: pipe-active %, top stalls, occupancy, and where local and global loads hit. A step chosen without that reading doesn't count. The sam3rs tracker skipped it and timed an FP16-accumulate QK^T, blamed on a spill; ncu later showed the head-dim-256 attention at one warp per scheduler, stalled on math throttle and waits, with the spill hitting L1, so more warps were the fix a faster MMA couldn't be (`~/code/research/inference/specialized-model-runtimes/sam3rs-decisions.md`)
- **Find out what the incumbent actually runs.** Read kernel names in nsys, not the flags you set. cuBLAS with a BF16 math mode on FP32 buffers still ran TF32, cuDNN's "TF32 mode" sometimes picked FP32-accurate algorithms, and its FP32 mode used Winograd. The library picks a different algorithm per device and batch. On GeForce, TF32 and FP32 share a peak, but tensor-core TF32 GEMMs reach it and FP32 SIMT GEMMs don't: an RTX 5090 ran 8192 GEMMs at 113 against 68 TFLOPS (1.65x, `~/code/research/inference/specialized-model-runtimes/docling-baselines-2026-11-01.md`). On datacenter parts TF32 is about 8x. TensorRT's fp16 engines run FP16-accumulate convs (`f16f16_f16f16_f16`), so judge them against the FP16-accumulate tensor peak: LivePortrait's TensorRT warp+SPADE sat 84% of the way to the FP32-accumulate floor, but ncu put it at 47.5% of its own path's peak (`~/code/research/inference/specialized-model-runtimes/liveportrait-baselines-2026-11-01.md`). (Lessons §2, §6, §7)
- **Judge accuracy against an audited reference.** Truth is f64 or the framework's own output, not the library. Audit the reference itself: SAM 3's "FP32" PyTorch cast its MLP to BF16, and NeMo references ran with dropout on. Gate on the end metric (WER, DER, score and IoU), and include low-confidence cases, because they move first. (Lessons §3, §7, §10)
- **Make parity gates fail loudly.** Assert output lengths, prefill outputs with NaN, never compare with `zip` or `f32::max` alone, and compare every output the user sees (boxes, kept queries, tokens, timestamps). Two ports had gates that passed on empty or NaN output. (Lessons §10)
- **Time only on an idle, dedicated GPU through the queue, by the playbook's acceptance rule.** In short: frozen binaries, paired ABBA runs in one job judged against A/A noise, every arm's output checked, winners re-confirmed. The full rule lives in `PW/playbooks/gpu-hill-climb.md` §4 step 5; link to it rather than restating it. The `gpu-ab` CLI applies it and prints ACCEPT, REJECT or INCONCLUSIVE; use it for every timed comparison rather than a hand-rolled script, and add an adapter for a new repo. Measured noise, refusals and design decisions are in `R/gpu-ab/README.md`. (Lessons §6, §7, §8)
- **Know how each profiler lies.** nsys inflates launch-bound work and needs `--cuda-graph-trace=node` for graphs. ncu locks base clocks and flushes L2 by default: pass `--clock-control none` and pick `--cache-control` by whether the working set stays in L2 across launches. These are settings to choose, not a reason to skip ncu. Where counters are denied (often on rented boxes), removal variants (delete math, staging or epilogue and re-time) bound a kernel's parts, but they don't replace ncu where it runs. A "pipe saturated" verdict is relative to the instruction issued. A single-process GPU-busy union doesn't bound throughput once processes share the GPU through MPS: grids under one wave run side by side (Docling passed its 64.5 pages/s serial bound at 70.4). Headline numbers come from untraced runs. Libraries push their own NVTX ranges inside yours (TensorRT's `myelinGraphExecute`), so give GPU time to the outermost harness range; innermost-wins moved 1.4 GPU-ms a frame out of LivePortrait's motion and landmark stages. (Lessons §6, §7, §8; `R/cuda-kernels/speakrs-rtx5090-optimization-2026-10-22.md`)
- **Re-bench at the final commit.** Correctness and safety fixes move speed: one SAM 3 fix took prompts from 12.0 to 15.8 ms. Quote only numbers from the final commit. (Lessons §7, §10)
- **Hybrid by default.** Keep the vendor library wherever it wins, try its epilogues and options before hand-written GEMMs (a cuda-oxide GEMM with a fused epilogue lost 1.5-2x to cuBLAS), and replace only measured hot spots. (Lessons §7, §8; no-cuDNN lessons)
- **Neither correctness nor speed carries over between GPU architectures.** Run sanitizers on at least two. A change tuned on one GPU either binds to that exact device or is checked on another before it ships in a shared PTX tier, and results say which GPU they were measured on. (Lessons §6; RTX 5090 note "Gating")
- **Get a correctness review against upstream between phases**, not only at the end, and not just a security scan. Ask advisors for designs with exposed fractions from traces, not "what next". (Lessons §10; `R/cuda-kernels/speakrs-cuda-advisor-comparison-2026-10-08.md` §8)
- **Write stop rules into the first brief**: attempt caps, acceptance criteria and known risks. Run long jobs as supervised tasks through the GPU queue, and consider handing a hard numerics problem to a different model after the second failed attempt (it worked once, in the speakrs tier run). (Lessons §4, §5)

## Kernels: cuda-oxide, shipped as PTX

Write every custom CUDA kernel in Rust with cuda-oxide, not CUDA C++, Triton or NVRTC source strings. cuBLAS, cuBLASLt and other vendor libraries are the only non-Rust device code, and a climb that needs a new kernel writes it in the kernel crate, not in a `.cu` file. cuda-oxide needs its pinned Rust toolchain (with rustc-dev), LLVM and a CUDA toolkit; that cost stays with the maintainers, and the published library builds on stable Rust:

- Take cuda-oxide from `praveenperera/cuda-rust` `master` at `b1e94cedf`, never a crates.io release, and use the toolchain that commit pins in its own `rust-toolchain.toml` (stable 1.99.1). Record the commit (a `rev` or the lockfile) so the PTX check knows which cuda-oxide built the committed files. Install cargo-oxide under the project's own `CARGO_HOME`, so other projects' backend caches stay at their revs. Move the pin in its own change, never inside a climb whose guards diff PTX: a move rebuilds every kernel.
- Kernels live in a standalone crate outside the workspace, with its own `rust-toolchain.toml` matching cuda-oxide's (the speakrs and sam3rs layout). Only a build command (an xtask or script run on a GPU box) compiles it, once per area and tier, to PTX and, where it pays, cubins.
- Commit the generated PTX and embed it with `include_str!` (`include_bytes!` for cubins). If the artifacts would pass the crates.io upload limit, split them into one crate per PTX tier: an sm75 baseline, plus higher tiers only where they unlock math the baseline can't use.
- The host library loads the PTX through the driver at run time (for example cudarc with dynamic loading), so users need no special toolchain, no CUDA toolkit and no GPU to build it, and the installed driver JIT-compiles the PTX.
- CI checks the committed PTX against hashes of every input that can change it (kernel sources, cuda-oxide rev, toolchain, toolkit), so stale PTX fails without a GPU.

Track D has the shipping details. Before writing or porting a kernel, read [references/cuda-oxide.md](references/cuda-oxide.md), the known traps and per-build checks. Don't edit it during a task: log each new trap you hit, and each slowdown you measured but couldn't explain, in the decision log with a `gotcha` Skill line. To compare a kernel build against a reference build, use this skill's scripts: [scripts/sass_mix.sh](scripts/sass_mix.sh) `REF.ptx NEW.ptx [ARCH] [KERNEL...]` (calls, registers, spills and trap opcodes per kernel; CPU only) and [scripts/nsys_kernel_diff.py](scripts/nsys_kernel_diff.py) `CTL.sqlite[,...] EXP.sqlite[,...] [N]` (per-kernel GPU time from nsys exports profiled through the queue). (`R/cuda-kernels/sam3rs-cuda-oxide-port-2026-10-30.md`; lessons §1)
